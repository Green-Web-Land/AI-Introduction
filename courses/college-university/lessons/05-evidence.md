# 5. Compare methods before conclusions

[Course home](../README.md) · [Curriculum](../CURRICULUM.md)

## Objective

Explain why differing records may not answer the same question.

## Explanation

Return to the [fictional packet](04-citations.md). Comparison needs more than counting how many records sound favourable. Ask what each measured, how participants were selected or assigned, and what is missing. Repeated summaries are not independent observations.

[Statistics Canada](../SOURCES.md) distinguishes association from causation. For our fictional non-random pilot, differences between people who chose rooms may have other explanations. We cannot conclude the room caused a difference just from those group means.

## Worked example

Evidence table:

| Record | Outcome | Design information | Important limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Completion time | Volunteers chose rooms | No random assignment or baseline |
| B | Repeats A | No new sample | Not independent |
| C | Comprehension score | Different task/group | Counts and assignment method missing |

A and C are not direct replications because the tasks and outcomes differ. Equal recorded means in C do not prove no possible effect in all settings.

## Practice

Evaluate: “Two papers prove Q is better, while C disproves them.” Identify three problems. Then write one cautious conclusion and one next question for a researcher or instructor.

## Answer and reasoning

Attempt the practice before reading this section.

B is not a second independent study; A's time outcome is not C's comprehension outcome; neither “proves” nor “disproves” is justified by this limited packet. Conclusion: “The records describe different outcomes with incomplete design information.” Next question: “How were C's groups formed, and how many participants were measured?” The packet does not provide that answer.

## Transfer task

Suppose a fictional appendix supplies C's participant count but still omits assignment method. What changes? One information gap closes; the remaining design question stays open. Do not turn a newly supplied detail into blanket confidence. Explain what additional evidence would be relevant without inventing it.
