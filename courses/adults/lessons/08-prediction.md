# Prediction: Your life with AI in X years

[Home](../README.md) · [Curriculum](../CURRICULUM.md)

Lesson AIE-08 · Draft · 2026-09-11

**Your goal:** describe a possible future, identify what it depends on and choose a useful action today.

Imagine looking back a year from now. What would you want to understand better? Which task would you like to do more confidently? What would you want to create or contribute?

AI could play a part in that future. Your practice, circumstances, other people and choices will also shape it. This lesson explores possibilities rather than promising an outcome.

## Read a prediction carefully

These are **author-created scenarios, not measured forecasts**. The dates organise our thinking; they are not deadlines for technological progress. We assign no probabilities, earnings estimates or guaranteed learning gains.

Separate three things:

- **Evidence:** something observed or supported by a source.
- **Assumption:** something that must hold for the scenario to make sense.
- **Possibility:** an outcome that could follow, but might not.

For example: “If I practise explaining a topic every week and check my mistakes, I may become better at explaining it.” Practice and checking are assumptions about your actions. Improvement is a possible result to measure, not a fact about your future.

## What does efficient use mean?

For this course, efficient use means choosing a suitable task and reaching a useful, checked result with a reasonable amount of effort. Count the time spent preparing, checking and repairing the output. A quick answer that creates more work is not necessarily an improvement.

Use AI when it helps. Use a calculator, a checklist, a book, another person or your own skill when those serve the task better. The aim is useful progress, not maximum time spent with AI.

## In one year: unfamiliar work may feel more approachable

**Assumptions:** you have access to a suitable tool, time to practise and a way to check answers. You choose small tasks and continue doing some work independently.

You might use AI to suggest a structure for a letter, create practice questions or help organise notes you supplied. You review the output, correct errors and keep examples that worked. A subject that once felt inaccessible may begin to look like a sequence of questions you can investigate.

**How you might see the world:** “I do not know this yet, but I know how to begin learning it.” More opportunities may become visible because you have a repeatable way to explore them.

**What could go wrong:** accepting finished answers without trying the task yourself may conceal gaps in understanding. Checking may take longer than doing a familiar task without AI.

**Check the scenario:** save a small starting example. Later, attempt a comparable task without AI. Compare accuracy, clarity and your ability to explain the result. Record total effort as well as speed.

## In three years: you may connect skills and contribute more

**Assumptions:** practice continues; you receive useful feedback; access remains affordable; and the people you work with permit the intended use.

You might combine an existing skill with better writing, analysis or project planning. A repair specialist could develop clearer instructions for customers. A community volunteer could turn approved notes into a guide that other volunteers improve. Neither needs to become an AI developer to contribute something useful.

**How you might see the world:** “My experience matters, and I have more ways to explain and apply it.” You may judge AI tools by whether they help with real needs rather than by impressive demonstrations.

**What could go wrong:** changing services, poor advice, limited time or workplace restrictions could interrupt progress. Employers and customers may value different skills than you expected. More output does not guarantee better work, employment or income.

**Check the scenario:** look for completed work that another person found useful. Ask what was unclear, what needed correction and whether you could maintain the result if the AI service disappeared.

## In five to ten years: several worlds are possible

Uncertainty grows at this distance. Technology alone will not determine education, employment, access or public trust. Consider these alternative conditions without ranking their likelihood:

| Possible conditions | What daily life might feel like | Preparation that could still help |
|---|---|---|
| Useful tools become broadly accessible, with effective safeguards and education. | More people can explore unfamiliar subjects and contribute to projects; checking and responsibility still matter. | Develop subject knowledge, verification habits and collaboration skills. |
| Capabilities improve, but access is uneven or concentrated in expensive services. | Some tasks become easier for people with access while others face barriers. | Keep portable notes, affordable alternatives and human learning networks. |
| Unreliable outputs, misuse or disappointing value limit adoption in some settings. | AI is used selectively; trusted human expertise and simpler tools remain central. | Learn to measure benefits, recognise failures and work independently. |

These conditions can coexist across different places and activities. No one path should be presented as inevitable.

**How you might see the world:** perhaps as a place where more things are possible—but where asking “Who benefits, who is excluded, and how do we know this works?” becomes part of good judgment.

## Different habits, different possibilities

| Approach | Possible benefit | Risk to notice |
|---|---|---|
| Thoughtful use: practise, verify and reflect. | You may build useful habits and produce work you understand. | Verification can still miss errors; effort and access are required. |
| Passive dependence: accept outputs and avoid independent practice. | Some immediate tasks may take less effort. | You may struggle to recognise mistakes or continue without the service. |
| Limited or chosen non-use: rely mainly on people and other tools. | You can still learn, create and develop strong skills. | Some AI-assisted opportunities may be less accessible; alternatives matter. |

These are behaviours and circumstances, not labels for people's intelligence or worth. This course should support choices, including deciding not to use AI. UNESCO's guidance emphasises human agency and inclusion in education; NIST identifies over-reliance as a risk in human–AI interaction. These principles inform the lesson, not its imagined timelines. [UNESCO guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=88737), [NIST Generative AI Profile, section 2.7](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)

## Help AI improve through useful feedback

People can contribute by describing failures accurately, providing safe examples and explaining whose needs a design misses. NIST's framework calls for mechanisms to incorporate reviewed feedback into system design and implementation. [NIST AI RMF Core, Govern 5.2](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)

Distinguish three activities:

1. **Correcting the current answer:** give the missing fact or explain the error so the conversation can continue more usefully. Check the revised result.
2. **Reporting a product problem:** use the provider's designated channel with a minimal, non-sensitive example. The provider may investigate, but a report does not promise a fix.
3. **Contributing to model improvement:** whether feedback is retained or used in later training depends on the service, its policies and your settings. Do not assume a correction permanently teaches the model or reaches every other user. Verify those details for the particular service before contributing data.

A useful fictional report says: “I supplied a table with three items. The summary omitted the third. Here is a synthetic table that reproduces the omission, the instructions and the expected result.” It describes the problem without sending a customer's original document.

Helping AI grow should mean improving usefulness, reliability, accessibility and human benefit. It does not require sharing private information, granting unnecessary access or accepting a harmful result.

## Exercise: write your own future

Choose a horizon: **one, three, five or ten years**. Complete this worksheet without an AI account:

| Question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| What would I like to understand, do or contribute? | |
| Which existing skill will I continue practising myself? | |
| Where might AI help with a small part of the work? | |
| What assumptions does this depend on? | |
| How could this future fail to happen? | |
| What will I do if access or reliability changes? | |
| What evidence of progress will I check? | |
| What one action can I take this week? | |

**Worked answer, fictional:** “In one year I want to explain basic gardening tasks clearly. I will practise one short explanation each week, sometimes ask AI to identify unclear wording, and check factual details against a reliable gardening reference. I need time, suitable references and useful feedback. If the tool is unavailable, I can ask a fellow gardener. Each month I will write an explanation unaided and ask a beginner which steps are unclear. This week I will write my starting example.”

This answer is useful because it identifies actions, assumptions, an alternative and observable progress. It does not promise mastery or require a future technological breakthrough.

## Check and revisit

Your scenario passes the exercise if it has a meaningful goal, explicit assumptions, a risk, a fallback and a measurable next step. There is no single correct imagined future.

At a review date you choose, compare your expectations with what happened. Record what changed your view. Revise the plan without rewriting the original prediction as if it had always been right. Learning to update your expectations is itself part of growing with AI.
