# 09 — Fair rules for AI

[Home](../README.md) · [Curriculum](../CURRICULUM.md) · [Word help](../GLOSSARY.md)

**Your mission:** Turn a principle into an action someone can check.

Use paper, drawing or spoken answers with a trusted adult. No AI account is needed.

## Design a classroom helper

Imagine a fictional tool that suggests reading activities. What rules would make it useful and fair?

We propose these principles: help people learn; protect privacy; treat people fairly; explain suggestions; keep meaningful human choices; and make it possible to ask for help or challenge a mistake. These are our proposals, not a claim that every country has one identical AI rulebook.

A principle needs action. “Respect privacy” is a good aim, but writing it in a prompt does not stop an application saving information.

## Four different things

- **Principle:** an idea about what ought to happen, such as fairness.
- **Law:** a requirement made through a legal system; adults must check which laws apply.
- **Product rule:** a service's conditions for using it.
- **Built-in protection:** a technical control, such as blocking a tool from sending messages.

They can support each other, but one does not replace the others.

## Your turn: improve the helper

For each problem, suggest an action and who should take responsibility.

1. The helper offers only tiny printed text.
2. It says every child must accept its first suggestion.
3. It asks children to post their full names and reading difficulties publicly.
4. It marks an answer wrong but gives no explanation or way to question it.

## Check together

1. The developer and educator could provide adjustable text and suitable alternative formats, then check whether they work for learners.
2. The educator could let children choose another activity or ask for a human explanation.
3. Adults and the provider should remove unnecessary collection and public posting. Children should not submit those details.
4. The educator should offer a way to review the answer with a person. Developers can help make explanations and correction routes available.

Children can point out a problem. Adults and providers remain responsible for fixing the system.

## Test a promise

A sign says: “Our helper is fair.” What would you look for?

Possible checks: activities in usable formats, a real choice to decline a suggestion, and someone who responds when a child reports a mistake. A sign alone proves none of these.

**Finish:** write one principle, one action and one check. For example: “Children can question an answer; the teacher offers a review; we can explain how to request it.”

Rules should help people. They should not demand that people obey an AI simply because it sounds confident.

All characters, situations and pretend AI responses in this lesson are invented teaching examples.
