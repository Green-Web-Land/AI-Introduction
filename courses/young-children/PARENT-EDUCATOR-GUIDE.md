# Parent and educator guide

Read this before the lessons. Approximately ages 6–8 is a starting audience choice, not evidence that every child will understand or enjoy every activity. Preview and adapt the material. A child may skip or stop without explaining why.

## Your role

Read the child-facing sections aloud if helpful. Keep the technical notes and external source pages for yourself. Sources may discuss harms in language unsuitable for this age group. None is a required child link or activity.

Use supplied fictional cards. Do not open a chatbot, put an AI toy into the activity or generate responses live. This course neither requires nor endorses a child account. If an adult separately considers a service, check its current terms, age rules, settings and suitability; this course supplies no permission and no guarantee.

Keep the session about a small distinction, not memorising facts about AI. Offer speech, pointing, drawing, objects drawn by the adult or another suitable equivalent. Do not force hearing, vision, handwriting or speech as the response method. A child may prefer ordinary story-making without technology.

## Protect the child's choices and privacy

Do not request names, addresses, passwords, photographs or real stories of harm. Do not make the child demonstrate a secret to show that they know what is private. Do not collect or publish their answers or pictures for this course.

Inclusion and privacy are responsibilities for adults and service operators; a child cannot secure a system by being careful or writing a privacy instruction. UNICEF's guidance informs those principles, but citing it does not certify this material. [S3](SOURCES.md)

## When a concern comes up

Keep fictional help practice neutral: a blank screen or puzzle with missing instructions. Do not show frightening content or stage a threat. If a child shares a real worry, stop the activity, listen calmly and avoid blame. Do not require them to keep interacting, collect screenshots or repeat details for the class.

Support them in reaching an appropriate trusted adult or qualified local service. If the first person does not help, another trusted adult can be approached. Use applicable safeguarding procedures and local emergency services when needed. This is not a reporting-law guide or an individual safeguarding assessment. Australian source services must not be assumed to operate in Canada or elsewhere. [S5–S6](SOURCES.md)

## What to notice, without scoring

| Lesson | A possible sign of understanding | If it is unclear |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Points to the person for care and help. | Compare the adult with the pencil; no machine-consciousness debate. |
| 2 | Chooses a title suggestion as a possible tool task. | Offer two choices rather than all three. |
| 3 | Continues the stated repeat with circle, then triangle. | Adult names or draws each shape; say the rule again. |
| 4 | Adds a detail about a pretend request. | Offer Leaf and Moon Garden as ready choices. |
| 5 | Checks the three-star card with support. | Count or represent the three items together. |
| 6 | Chooses a made-up setting without giving private details. | Return to cloud castle or moon garden. |
| 7 | Offers a way for each character to join or decline. | Discuss the fictional characters only. |
| 8 | Points to help or uses a help phrase. | Model the phrase; do not insist on role-play. |
| 9 | Identifies one idea kept or changed. | The adult can write the child's chosen fictional explanation. |
| 10 | Describes a possibility rather than a certainty. | Compare tool and no-tool story-making. |

Final cards: T1 counts three stars with help; T2 uses a fictional setting instead of an address; T3 seeks a trusted grown-up. Revisiting is welcome. There is no pass mark, certificate, comparison among children or claim that these observations predict real-world behaviour.

## Limits of the teaching models

The pattern row is a deliberately simple repeating rule, not an AI training demonstration. The wrong-answer card is fiction, not a measured product failure. The future stories are scenarios, not expert forecasts. “AI is not a person” does not require a child to settle philosophical questions; it prevents treating a tool as a caregiver.

No source establishes this exact ten-lesson course as effective or safe for every child. Learner feedback, if later proposed, would need its own appropriately reviewed method rather than informal collection through GitHub.
