# Your next 30 days

**Your choices matter. Learn. Question. Decide.**

Lesson 8 of 8 · Adult beginners · About 35–45 minutes for the final case and plan

**By the end:** you can combine the course's questions into a reasoned proposal, choose a manageable learning goal and decide what to keep or change after practice.

Thirty days is a convenient planning window. You can stretch it, shorten it or pause. The aim is a useful next step that fits your life.

## Begin with something you care about

Lina wants to explain a workshop update clearly. Arun, a community volunteer, wants to turn a short list of confirmed event details into a useful notice. They have different responsibilities but can practise the same habit.

“Become excellent at AI” gives neither person a clear next action. “Write a notice that preserves all the supplied facts and marks missing information” is something they can attempt and check.

## Learn — choose a small goal

Start with something you already understand enough to evaluate. Your goal might involve writing, organising information, learning a topic or making something creative.

Write down:

- **The task:** what you want to do.
- **The evidence:** what you will check it against.
- **The skill:** what you want to remain able to do yourself.

For Arun: “I will turn supplied event notes into a clear notice, compare every detail with the notes and explain my changes without reopening the sample answer.”

This course's plan is a practice aid, not a promise about how fast someone learns.

## Question — decide what would count as progress

A polished page can hide mistakes. So can a familiar exercise that you remember. Plan to try a changed example as well as the first one.

Ask:

1. Did I preserve the facts and notice what was missing?
2. Can I explain my decisions in my own words?
3. Does this help with a task that matters to me?
4. What time, support or access do I need?

Keep only useful learning notes. You can use the supplied invented material throughout.

## Decide — make a flexible plan

| When | Small action | What to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–7 | Try one task yourself and compare it with the supplied facts | A starting example and one question |
| Days 8–14 | Study feedback; revise the task using a person, reference or optional AI assistance | A correction and the reason for it |
| Days 15–21 | Try a different example, then explain your work without the sample | Evidence of what you can now do |
| Days 22–30 | Review usefulness, effort and remaining difficulty | A decision to continue, change, pause or stop |

Choose a pace with room for the rest of your life. If a tool is unavailable, you can compare with the answer guidance or ask a willing supporter. An independent explanation can be spoken or written.

## Worked plan — Arun's event notice

Arun chooses two short practice sessions each week. The number is his preference, not a requirement.

His starting attempt includes a booking claim that the notes do not support. He marks it and asks, “How can I make unknown details clear?” After studying an example, he replaces the claim with a question for the organiser.

Later, he writes from a different fact card without looking at the earlier answer. He checks the date, time, place, cost and missing information. At the end of the month, he decides whether this practice has made his notices easier to check.

If he keeps making the same mistake, he can narrow the task to separating known and unknown details. That is useful information for choosing the next practice.

## Your turn — build and test your plan

Complete the [30-day worksheet](../WORKSHEETS.md#my-next-30-days). Name a task, one check, a source of help, a fresh example and a reason you might change course.

Then try this fictional starting task:

> Riverside reading circle: 18 August, 14:00–14:45, Oak Room. Bring one book. Entry is free. The organiser has not supplied booking information.

Write a two-sentence notice. Keep missing booking information visible. Compare your draft before reading the example.

### Compare your reasoning

One answer: “Join the free Riverside reading circle on 18 August, 14:00–14:45, in Oak Room and bring one book. Booking information is still to be confirmed.”

Check the five supplied details and the unresolved booking question. Different wording works if it preserves them. In a real announcement, a year and full location may also be needed.

## Try a different situation

The organiser now confirms: “Book in advance; the meeting moves to Pine Room and starts at 15:00. The date, free entry and book instruction stay the same.” No revised finish time is given.

Write an updated notice. What new uncertainty should you notice?

### Compare your reasoning

Use 18 August, 15:00, Pine Room, free entry, bring one book, and advance booking. The finish time is now unconfirmed: do not automatically keep 14:45 or invent 15:45.

One answer: “Join the free Riverside reading circle on 18 August at 15:00 in Pine Room; book in advance and bring one book. The finish time is still to be confirmed.”

This changed example checks whether you reconsider the facts when conditions change.

## Bring the course together

Use this final fictional case. It brings together a useful task, a shared service and a learning goal:

> Riverside wants to use AI to group suggestions for new community classes. Staff spend about two hours each week grouping them manually. Suggestions arrive through an app and on paper. The centre has not measured the time needed to check AI groupings. Some suggestions include personal details and access needs. The organiser proposes trying the idea before making a long-term decision.

Prepare a short proposal using the [final worksheet](../WORKSHEETS.md#bringing-it-together). You can write six sentences or explain them aloud:

1. Name one useful task to try and a clear boundary.
2. Describe information suitable for the first practice.
3. Identify a possible benefit and a burden to examine.
4. Name a perspective that should be included.
5. Propose a fair comparison and a skill someone could practise.
6. State a review point and evidence that could change your recommendation.

There is no requirement to recommend AI. Give reasons for your proposal before opening the example.

### Compare your reasoning

One proposal is: “Try grouping invented suggestions while keeping every suggestion visible; do not choose classes or send announcements. Practise on fictional cards before deciding how actual information may be handled. A possible benefit is easier sorting, but missing requests and checking time need examination. Include people who use paper and staff who arrange accessible activities. Compare manual and assisted grouping on the same invented set, counting checking and corrections as well as drafting, and practise explaining the categories. Review after two small practice sessions; pause or revise if important requests disappear or the effort is not worthwhile.”

The review point is an editorial choice for this example. An equally reasonable proposal could improve the manual process first, or postpone the experiment until review time is available. Support your choice with the supplied facts and make uncertainties explicit. A successful fictional exercise alone would not justify deployment with actual participant information.

## Your final choice

Finish your plan with: “I will try ___ because ___. I will check ___ on ___. If ___, I will change ___.”

You decide what is useful enough to continue. Learning may lead you to use AI, use it differently, ask for better support or choose another method. The point is greater understanding and a choice you can explain.
